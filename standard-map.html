A short explanation

We start with the first Fourier mode, and may add further integer modes:

\[ V_{\mathbf K,\alpha}(x)=-\sum_{n\geq1}\frac{K_n}{(2\pi n)^\alpha}\cos(2\pi n x). \]

Keeping only the first mode, with its parameter \(K_1\), gives the usual Chirikov standard map. Type an integer \(1\leq n\leq99\) and press \(+\) to add \(K_n\).

The exponent \(\alpha\) controls the decay of the higher-frequency coefficients. It can be adjusted according to the chosen frequencies to make either the KAM portrait or the \(\beta\)-graph easier to read. Auto chooses a practical experimental balance from the selected Fourier modes.

The corresponding dynamics are given by the 2D map

\[ f(x,y)=\bigl(x+y+V'(x)\pmod 1,\;y+V'(x)\bigr). \]

For a general potential \(V\), the function \(\beta_V(\omega)\) records the smallest average action among orbits with rotation number \(\omega\):

\[ \beta_V\!\left(\frac pq\right)=\frac1q\min_{x_q=x_0+p}\sum_{n=0}^{q-1} \left[\frac12(x_{n+1}-x_n)^2+V(x_n)\right]. \]

The left window is a phase portrait: every coloured trace follows one initial point under the map. A regular curve confines the orbit; gaps or island chains show how this organisation can break down. In particular, the loss of a rational invariant curve has a variational trace: \(\beta_V\) can develop a corner at the corresponding rational rotation number.

The right window shows the change \(\beta_V-\beta_0\) from the unperturbed system. Click \(\partial_\omega\) in its title to switch to its derivative; dashed joins are shown only when a slope jump is a strong local outlier of the numerical data, so they are candidates rather than certificates of a corner. The subscript \(0\) means the zero potential \(V=0\): this reference map is integrable, and \(\beta_0(\omega)=\omega^2/2\) is analytic. The summands in the definition above are called actions: they play a role analogous to that of distance for geodesics or chord length for billiard trajectories.

More generally, the regularity of \(\beta_V\) is closely related to the integrability of the dynamics. Thus the graph records both how the potential changes the least action needed to realise each average rotation and, through possible corners, how invariant curves can be lost.

The question we are interested in is:

Can one deduce \(V\) from \(\beta\)?

This inverse problem, in the spirit of Kac's celebrated question “Can one hear the shape of a drum?”, is treated in this paper.
